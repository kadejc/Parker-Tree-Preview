Questions about your tree’s health

Reviewed by Ryder Capps, ISA Certified Arborist

How can I tell if my tree is dying?

Common warning signs include large dead or leafless branches during the growing season, bark falling away from the trunk, mushrooms or fungus at the base, deep cracks or cavities, sudden leaning, and leaves that wilt, yellow, or drop early. One sign alone does not always mean a tree is dying. An assessment helps separate stress from decline.

Is my tree dead or just dormant?

In winter many healthy trees look bare. Gently scratch a small twig with a fingernail: green, moist tissue underneath usually means it is alive, while brown, dry, brittle wood suggests that branch is dead. Check several branches in different parts of the canopy, or ask us to take a look.

What does oak wilt look like?

On live oaks, leaves often show yellow or brown veins (veinal necrosis) and the tree drops leaves through much of the year. On red oaks, leaves can wilt and turn brown in a few weeks, usually starting at the top of the tree. Oak wilt spreads through connected roots and by sap beetles to fresh wounds, so suspected cases are worth an early assessment.

What should I do after a hard freeze?

Be patient. Many trees that look dead after a hard freeze leaf out late in spring. Wait until new growth shows which branches died before pruning, then remove only the dead wood. Use the scratch test on twigs to check for living green tissue.

Does a struggling tree always need feeding?

No. Pale leaves, visible veins, insects, or fungal symptoms can have different causes. We assess the problem before discussing nutrients, a targeted treatment, or another approach. See tree feeding.

How do you decide whether to treat or remove a tree?

We assess the tree and explain the options. Our goal is to help you understand when treatment could make a difference and when removal is the best course.

What should I tell you about my tree?

Tell us what you are seeing and where the tree is located. Those details help start the conversation; we can then discuss an assessment and next steps.
